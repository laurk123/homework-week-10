Hong Kong’s Importance in International Finance Since 1997
Hong Kong is an important place for banking, investment and international business.
Companies use its markets to raise money and investors use them to buy shares and other
financial products. Its connection to mainland China is a major reason for its importance.
However, Hong Kong also has its own currency and a different legal system. This makes it
useful to businesses that want to work with China through a system they know better and have
more faith in. Since the handover in 1997, Hong Kong has kept many of its financial systems
while building stronger links with the mainland. These links have created opportunities but they
have also brought risks for the outsiders.
Hong Kong’s financial system was already developed before the 1997 handover. One
important part was the Linked Exchange Rate System which began in 1983. This made it so the
Hong Kong dollar is pegged to the United States dollar. The arrangement continued after Britain
handed Hong Kong back to China in 1997 (Hong Kong Monetary Authority [HKMA], 2024).
Thanks to it, businesses did not have to start using a new currency or adjust to a completely
different exchange rate system. This helped keep things more stable during a major political
change. Although Hong Kong's government changed, many of the systems used by banks and
businesses remained in place.
The Basic Law helped maintain these systems. It sets out the main rules for how Hong
Kong is governed. Article 8 allows common law and other existing laws to continue with certain
conditions. Article 109 says that the government should support Hong Kong's position as an
international financial centre. Articles 110-112 cover its financial policies, its currency and the
free movement of money (Basic Law of the Hong Kong Special Administrative Region of the
People’s Republic of China, 1990, arts. 8, 109–112). These rules are important for businesses.
Before investing, people need to know how their money, investments and properties will be
protected. They also need to know whether they can move their money into and out of Hong
Kong without any restrictions.
The link to the United States dollar supports Hong Kong's international business. Under
the present system, one United States dollar is worth between 7.75 HKD and 7.85 HKD (HKMA,
2024). This keeps the exchange rate within a small range. It makes planning easier for
companies that use both currencies because they face less uncertainty about exchange rate
changes. Also, having the Hong Kong dollar pegged to USD has its flaws: high United States
interest rates can make borrowing expensive in Hong Kong. Because the Hong Kong dollar is
linked to the US dollar, local interest rates follow US rates, although they are also affected by
local market conditions. This can be a problem when the two economies are in different
economic situations. For example, the United States may keep rates high to slow down the
price increases and inflation, while Hong Kong's businesses could benefit from cheaper loans.
Higher borrowing costs can make companies delay their expansions and leave households with
less money to spend if their mortgages rise. Hong Kong cannot freely lower interest rates to
support its own local economy while keeping both the currency link and the free movement of
money. The system therefore offers a more stable exchange rate, but gives Hong Kong less
freedom to respond to local economic problems.
After 1997, Hong Kong’s financial connections with mainland China grew stronger. One
important example was the wider use of the renminbi, China’s currency. In 2004 banks in Hong
Kong were allowed to offer personal renminbi accounts. This meant that customers could keep
money in renminbi in a Hong Kong bank account. In 2009, a trial programme allowed certain
cross-border trade payments to be made in renminbi (Cockerell & Shoory, 2012). Businesses
covered by the programme could therefore use Chinese currency to pay for goods traded
across borders. These links later grew beyond bank accounts and trade payments. The Stock
Connect programmes gave overseas investors a way to buy selected mainland Chinese shares
through Hong Kong. For example, an international investment fund could use a broker in Hong
Kong to buy shares listed in Shanghai or Shenzhen. Shanghai - Hong Kong Stock Connect
opened in November 2014, followed by Shenzhen - Hong Kong Stock Connect in December
2016. The programmes work in both directions. Mainland investors can also buy selected
shares listed in Hong Kong. This gives investors a bigger choice and exposure to both mainland
and Hong Kong markets. Bond Connect created a similar system for bonds in July 2017. At first
the programme allowed overseas investors to trade bonds in mainland China’s interbank bond
market. In 2021 a second channel opened, allowing mainland investors to trade bonds outside
the mainland through Hong Kong (Hong Kong Exchanges and Clearing Limited, 2024).
Together, these programmes made Hong Kong a more important link between mainland
markets and international markets. They also show that Hong Kong's financial role depends on
cooperation with mainland China. Hong Kong cannot decide by itself which mainland markets
should be opened to Hongkongers and foreign investors. The authorities on both sides need to
agree on how the connection works. This creates more opportunities for Hong Kong, but it also
means that part of its success depends on decisions made in mainland China. Its separate
system is useful because it connects markets with different rules. If these differences
disappeared or the connection became less useful, Hong Kong would need to do something
else to attract foreign investors and money.
Finance is also a large part of Hong Kong's own economy. In 2024, financial services
accounted for 26.2% of its GDP and employed around 265,000 people, or 7.2% of total
employment (Census and Statistics Department, 2025). This includes banking, insurance, stock
brokerage, asset management, and other financial services. These figures show that finance is
a large part of Hong Kong's economy while employing a much smaller share of workers
compared to other key industries. Therefore, its success matters to the whole economy, even
when most of the people in Hong Kong work in different fields. A strong financial sector does not
automatically mean that everyone has the same opportunities. This difference is important when
looking at everyday life in Hong Kong. The city can attract international investors and funds
while local people still struggle with the cost of living. The International Monetary Fund (IMF,
2025) identifies housing affordability as a continuing problem which adds to inequality. This does
not mean that finance alone causes expensive housing. However, it shows that financial
success and a comfortable life for residents are not always the same thing. Someone earning a
high salary in finance may experience the city completely differently from someone in a
lower-paid job. Government policies still matter in helping more people benefit from economic
growth. The closer connections with mainland China also bring risks. The IMF (2025) describes
Hong Kong's financial system as strong, but warns that bigger mainland links increase its
exposure to problems within China's economy. These include difficulties in the real estate
market and tensions between countries. Connections that bring investment into Hong Kong can
also carry financial problems. This does not mean that Hong Kong will lose its financial
importance. However, it means that banks and regulators need to watch and weigh these risks
carefully. Access to a larger market creates opportunities but relying heavily on that market also
makes it harder to avoid its problems.
Trust is another important part of Hong Kong's financial position. Businesses need to
believe that contracts will be respected and that legal disputes will be handled fairly. Written
laws are important, but investors also look at how these laws work in practice. A company
putting their funds into a business for several years needs confidence that the rules will be
applied consistently and they won't be changed. Hong Kong's separate legal system can help
provide the confidence needed for outsiders. Its value therefore comes from more than access
to China. It also comes from providing an environment where international businesses feel
comfortable making decisions and keeping their investments.
Hong Kong has also tried to develop newer areas of finance. In June 2023, a new
licensing system began for centralised virtual asset trading platforms, including platforms used
to trade cryptocurrencies. The Securities and Futures Commission (2023b) explained that
licensed platforms could serve ordinary investors under rules designed to protect them. These
included checks on customers, platform management and the assets available for trading. This
shows how Hong Kong is trying to attract new financial businesses while keeping them under
supervision. It also creates another reason for firms to consider Hong Kong when deciding
where to operate. However, attracting centralized crypto businesses is only one part of the
ecosystem. Tokenised securities remain subject to the existing rules for traditional securities. In
its November 2023 guidance, the Securities and Futures Commission (2023a) explained that
intermediaries must also manage the additional risks created by tokenisation, including risks
involving ownership records, cybersecurity and blockchain networks. The required controls
depend on the technology and activities involved. This connects newer financial services with
the older foundations of Hong Kong’s success. Whether a business deals with shares, bonds or
digital assets, trust and reliable rules remain important. New technology may change the
products being traded, but it does not remove the need for investor protection.
Overall, Hong Kong’s importance in finance has developed through both continuity and
change since 1997. Its own currency and separate legal system continued, while its financial
links with mainland China became stronger. These links created opportunities for banks,
companies and investors, but also increased exposure to mainland economic problems and
political decisions. The growth of finance has not removed the difficulties faced by all residents
either. Hong Kong’s future depends on keeping the services and institutions that make it useful
to international business. Its connection to China is important, but the differences between their
systems are also part of what makes Hong Kong valuable.
